Brains, classrooms, and markets.

In a fast moving AI world, I keep coming back to these three domains: three places where learning happens, information moves, and choices made now, even small ones, could profoundly shape society's future.

I’m Raaghav, a recent Caltech graduate in computer science and economics. My work has moved between neural computation, tools for learning in the age of AI, and the systems that distribute resources such as attention and expertise. I’m curious about what these fields can teach one another, and about building things that make those connections useful.

Now

working on models for neuromorphic navigation

The brain remains our clearest example of intelligence that is adaptive, efficient, and grounded in the world. As artificial systems approach and exceed human capabilities in more domains, understanding how we think and what distinguishes human intelligence from artificial intelligence becomes increasingly important. Neuroscience can offer principles for building AI that is safer and more interpretable, while unprecedented advances in neural recording, computation, and intervention today are also making it possible to understand brain disorders more mechanistically and translate that understanding into better treatments and technologies. I believe understanding the brain is one of science’s final frontiers, and perhaps its most human one.

Education has an outsized effect on how people think, collaborate, and solve problems, while unequal access to good education remains a major source of socioeconomic inequality. AI is now changing both the skills that matter and the ways those skills can be developed. That makes this a critical moment to rethink education itself, not simply add new tools to old systems. At the same time, it creates an unprecedented opportunity to expand access to high-quality learning and help reduce long-standing inequalities in who gets to benefit from it.

Markets determine how scarce resources such as money, attention, information, and expertise move through society. As AI takes on a larger role in economic activity and decision-making, these systems will increasingly shape who gets what, when, and why. That makes this a critical moment to rethink how resources are allocated, and to use better economic and network design to make those systems both more efficient and more fair.

Theory · 2024–present

Navigation with noisy sensors

Optimal-control and graph-kernel views of neuromorphic navigation, including why Gaussian-like place fields may be useful.

This work develops Laplacian formulations of Endotaxis, proves convergence properties, and compares the method with successor representations.

How Endotaxis represents a map

Point-cell input encodes the agent’s current node. It feeds recurrent map cells whose connections store graph adjacency; the resulting goal signal can then be greedily increased to choose each next step.

Goal signals on a ring

The Laplacian network produces a smooth goal gradient across the ring, while the adjacency network concentrates activity close to the goal. The broader gradient remains useful when activity is noisy.

Goal signals on a branched graph

The Laplacian signal changes more continuously with graph distance. Greedy ascent therefore follows a direction that more closely approximates a shortest path than the localized adjacency signal.

Research · 2024

Psychology-guided social reasoning in LLMs

Multi-agent experiments testing whether psychological reasoning priors help language models negotiate, cooperate, and resolve conflict more believably.

Using the SOTOPIA benchmark, the project measured believability, relationship quality, goal persistence, and emotion-aware decision making. Psychology-guided agents showed stronger social alignment, personality formation, and empathetic reasoning.

Research · 2024

Semantic embeddings for neural decoding

A group project building SYNAPSE: an LLM pipeline that extracts semantic structure from natural video and generates parallel embeddings for testing what is encoded in neural activity.

Semantic extraction produces a family of semantically relevant embeddings rather than a single fixed representation. Those embeddings can be tested in parallel for evidence of neural coding using multiple plug-in neural decoding models against single-neuron responses during movie viewing.

Question · brains + markets

What is one more observation worth?

An animal can look again, a model can request more context, and a teacher can ask an expert. In each case, information has a cost. I’m interested in when the next observation changes the decision enough to be worth paying for.
